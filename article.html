Why the next decade belongs to the patient builders

Viren Kevat 12 March 2025 8 min read Leadership
Share
Topic Leadership

In 2002 I was asked, by someone I respect a great deal, how quickly Nexora could reach profitability. I gave an honest answer: not for a while. The conversation ended politely, and the funding did not arrive. It was one of the most useful rejections of my career.

Twenty-three years later, I am convinced that the defining advantage in technology is no longer speed. Speed has been commoditised. Anyone can ship a prototype in a weekend. What remains rare — and therefore valuable — is the willingness to stay with a hard problem long enough to solve it properly.

The tyranny of the quarter

Public markets reward predictability, and predictability pushes leaders towards the next ninety days. That is not a criticism; it is simply the arithmetic of the system. But it means that the investments with the longest payback — security architecture, research, people, trust — are always the first to be questioned.

The leaders I admire most do not fight this pressure by ignoring it. They answer it with a clear, published long-term plan, so that investors can see the quarter as one step on a visible road rather than a verdict on the whole journey.

Patience is not slowness. It is the refusal to let urgency make decisions that only time can justify.

What patient builders do differently

After three decades, I see the same habits in the companies that endure:

  • They write down the ten-year plan — and revisit it every year, not every quarter.
  • They protect a research budget that cannot be raided when a quarter looks tight.
  • They hire for curiosity, because the skills required in year ten do not exist yet.
  • They treat trust as an asset that compounds, and every broken promise as a withdrawal.

Patience in the age of AI

Nowhere is this more important than in artificial intelligence. The temptation to deploy first and understand later is enormous, and the cost of getting it wrong is borne not by us, but by the people who depend on our systems.

At Nexora we publish the limitations of every AI system we ship, we assign each model a named human owner, and we are prepared to delay a launch when the evidence is not yet strong enough. It is slower. It is also the only approach that has ever earned us the right to run critical infrastructure for banks, hospitals and governments.

Key takeaways

  • Speed is abundant; endurance is scarce — and endurance is the real moat.
  • Publish a long-term plan so the quarter is read in context.
  • Protect research, people and trust from short-term budget pressure.
  • In AI, being responsible and being first are not opposites — but responsible must come first.

A closing thought

I sometimes think about that conversation in 2002. The funding that did not arrive forced us to earn every customer, one trusted delivery at a time. Had it come, I suspect we would have grown faster and built less. Patience, in the end, was not something we chose. It was something that chose us — and I am grateful for it.

If you are building something today, my advice is simple: decide what you want to be proud of in ten years, and let that, rather than the next quarter, set the pace.

Viren Kevat

Chairman & CEO of Nexora Technologies. Writes about leadership, responsible AI and building companies that last.
